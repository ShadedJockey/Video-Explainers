## 1. Heroes, then and now
[tag: Heroes]

> People have told hero stories for as long as they have told stories.
> One of the oldest, the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, was written in Mesopotamia about four thousand years ago.
> The Greeks had Achilles, medieval Europe had its knights, and we have films and games.
> The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung called the hero an **archetype**: a pattern that keeps reappearing.
> In 1949, the American mythologist Joseph Campbell published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*.
> By faces, he meant people: each hero is another instance of the same figure.
> The *Legend of Zelda* games show this in miniature: in each era, Link is a new person, and still the hero.
> Every hero story is a kind of map for facing the unknown.
> Campbell drew a map of those maps: the hero leaves home, is tested, and returns changed.

## 2. What heroes share
[tag: Qualities]

> Across cultures, heroes share qualities: courage, persistence, care for others, and a willingness to face what others avoid.
> But why would cultures that never met keep inventing the same figure?

## 3. Convergent evolution
[tag: Convergence]

> Biology has a name for this kind of pattern: **convergent evolution**.
> Distantly related species that face the same problem often reach similar solutions.
> Insects, birds and bats each evolved wings, and octopuses and humans each evolved camera-like eyes.
> Cultures converge too: writing was invented separately in Mesopotamia, China and Mesoamerica.
> The hero may be another example.
> Every culture has problems and benefits from people who can solve them, so stories about those people keep being invented.

## 4. Correcting errors
[tag: Knowledge]

> What makes someone good at solving problems?
> The philosopher Karl Popper argued that knowledge grows by finding and correcting errors.
> A hero does this reliably, in themselves, and better than the culture around them.
> In Hayao Miyazaki's 1984 film *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind*, everyone fears a spreading toxic jungle.
> Nausicaä studies it, and finds that it is cleaning the poisoned soil.
> Her judgement isn't perfect, but it is reliable enough to face problems no one predicted, in familiar places and unknown ones.

## 5. Reach
[tag: Reach]

> In *The Beginning of Infinity*, the physicist David Deutsch argues that problems are soluble, given the right knowledge.
> A hero treats all problems alike: a monster, a famine and a disease are all things to understand.
> That is the hero's **reach**.

## 6. Rediscovered and improved
[tag: Improvement]

> The hero is often rediscovered, and often improved.
> Gilgamesh was a great warrior, but his epic opens with him oppressing his own city.
> In Homer's *Iliad*, Achilles is the greatest fighter at Troy, yet his pride keeps him from battle while his friends die.
> Many past heroes were heroic in one domain and not in others.
> Later stories keep what worked and correct what did not.

## 7. Infinite faces
[tag: Infinite Faces]

> Campbell counted a thousand faces.
> But if a hero is anyone who corrects their own errors and treats every problem as soluble, the number has no limit.
