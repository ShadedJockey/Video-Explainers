# Narration: The Hero with a Thousand Faces

**Status:** DRAFT v2, awaiting approval (pipeline step 2). §7 approved. v2 swaps in your montage heroes.
**Target:** 2:30 at about 150 words per minute (about 375 words)
**Main argument:** the hero's journey lasts because it's a picture of problem-solving. A problem appears, we try a solution, we're sometimes wrong, and we try again. The hero is the archetype of greatness in the face of problems: not giving up hope or will. (Popper and Deutsch; see `sources.md` §1b.)
**Scope:** the hero idea, its many forms in media, and the philosophy around it. Whether Campbell influenced any given work is beside the point, so the narration makes no influence claims.

Fact IDs in brackets point to rows in `sources.md`. They're for checking only and aren't spoken.

## 2. The hero with a thousand faces (0:10 to 0:28)

> In 1949, Joseph Campbell published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. Myths everywhere, he argued, share one hero wearing different faces: leave home, face trials, return changed. Carl Jung called recurring figures like this archetypes. At its core: a problem, and someone who won't give up.

## 6. The supporting cast (1:43 to 2:04)

> No hero solves problems alone. The mentor passes on knowledge: Merlin, Gandalf, Krishna, the Great Deku Tree. The shadow is the problem given a face: Ganondorf, Vader, Ravana. The trickster breaks the rules and exposes mistakes: Loki, Anansi, Sun Wukong. And the wise guide lights the way: Princess Zelda, or Guanyin.

## 7. A thousand faces (2:04 to 2:25), approved

> The philosopher Karl Popper argued that all life is problem solving. David Deutsch put it in two lines: problems are inevitable; problems are soluble. Maybe that's why the thousand faces feel like one. The hero is what greatness looks like against a problem: wrong sometimes, tested always, and never out of hope.

*Facts:* [P1] [P2] [P3]. The final line is the video's argument (§1b).
*Key captions:* **"All life is problem solving." (Karl Popper)** · **"Problems are inevitable. Problems are soluble." (David Deutsch)**
