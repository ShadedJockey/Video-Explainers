# Narration: The Hero with a Thousand Faces

**Status:** DRAFT v1, awaiting approval (pipeline step 2)
**Target:** 2:30 at about 150 words per minute (about 375 words)
**Main argument:** the hero's journey lasts because it's a picture of problem-solving. A problem appears, we try a solution, we're sometimes wrong, and we try again. The hero is the archetype of greatness in the face of problems: not giving up hope or will. (Popper and Deutsch; see `sources.md` §1b.)

## 2. The hero with a thousand faces (0:10 to 0:30)

> In 1949, Joseph Campbell published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. His claim: myths everywhere share one hero wearing different faces. Leave home. Face trials. Return changed. Carl Jung called figures like this archetypes. But strip the pattern down, and something simpler remains: a problem, and someone who won't give up on it.

## 3. The Hero of Time (0:30 to 0:55)

> Take Link in *Ocarina of Time*, from 1998. He's an ordinary boy from the forest, until the Great Deku Tree calls him to adventure. He draws the Master Sword and wakes seven years later, in a Hyrule fallen to darkness. And like every player, he falls, fails and tries again. Across the series, the hero keeps returning in new forms: a hero with many faces.

## 4. Ancient roots (0:55 to 1:15)

> Nearly four thousand years ago, Gilgamesh set out to defeat death. His solution failed, and he came home changed. Heracles faced his labours, and when two didn't count, he did two more. Odysseus spent ten years fighting his way home. And only the true king could pull the sword from the stone. Sound familiar?

*Facts:* [A1] [A2] [A4] [A5] [A6]
*Key captions:* **"Gilgamesh, c. 1800 BCE"** · **"12 Labours"** · **"The Odyssey, c. 700 BCE"** · **"Sword in the Stone ↔ Master Sword"** (split screen)
*Note:* Gilgamesh is the clearest "mistaken solution" in the video. Immortality fails, and he finds a new answer in what he leaves behind.

## 6. The supporting cast (1:45 to 2:08)

> No hero solves problems alone. The mentor passes on knowledge: Merlin, Gandalf, Krishna, the Great Deku Tree. The shadow is the problem given a face: Ganondorf, Darth Vader, Ravana. The trickster breaks the rules and exposes our mistakes: Loki, Anansi, Sun Wukong. And the wise guide lights the way, like Princess Zelda, or Guanyin in *Journey to the West*.

## 7. A thousand faces (2:08 to 2:25)

> The philosopher Karl Popper argued that all life is problem solving. David Deutsch put it in two lines: problems are inevitable; problems are soluble. Maybe that's why the thousand faces feel like one. The hero is what greatness looks like against a problem: wrong sometimes, tested always, and never out of hope.

*Facts:* [P1] [P2] [P3]. The final line is the video's argument (§1b).
*Key captions:* **"All life is problem solving." (Karl Popper)** · **"Problems are inevitable. Problems are soluble." (David Deutsch)**
*Visual:* the mosaic face assembles from every cutout, finishing on "never out of hope".

## Changes from the outline

- **Main argument added:** heroes as problem-solvers (Popper, Deutsch). It's set up in §2 ("a problem, and someone who won't give up on it"), threaded through §3 (game over, try again), §4 (Gilgamesh's failed solution, Heracles' re-done labours) and §5 ("stumbles, and tries again"), and paid off in §7.
- **§3:** "crosses into a darker world" became "wakes seven years later, in a Hyrule fallen to darkness". "Link's spirit is reborn" became "the hero keeps returning in new forms".
- **§4:** "Gilgamesh, from the oldest surviving epic" became "nearly four thousand years ago". Arthur is the sword in the stone, not Excalibur.
- **§5:** only Lucas gets "drew on Campbell". Everyone else is shown as fitting the pattern.
- **§6:** "wise guide" is used as our own label.
- **§7:** "leaving the familiar, being tested, and coming back different" became the Popper/Deutsch payoff, keeping "tested".
