# The Hero With Infinite Faces: Running Ideas

The core thesis behind the video: the Hero is a kind of universal knowledge, and heroes are the people who carry it. Presented as the video's own conjecture, not a claim by Campbell, Popper or Deutsch.

Last updated: 25 November 2026

## The thesis

Campbell said there is one hero with a thousand faces. He was right that there is one, and wrong about what it is. The Hero is not a plot, a sequence of stages or an inherited archetype. It is knowledge: hope, fallibilism, curiosity, a critical attitude and the virtues that let a mind face the unfamiliar. Running in a mind, it keeps the cycle of problem, guess, error and correction going. Anyone, in any world, can carry it, so its faces are infinite.

## Map and territory

Stories are maps of heroism; heroes are the territory. Myth and story are one subject, the general theory of heroes is another, and the video names the split early and keeps it clean.

Campbell's stages are shadows of the cycle on the map:

- Leaving home is facing an unfamiliar problem.
- The ordeal is the error.
- The return is bringing the new knowledge back to others.

The recurring figures are shadows too:

- **The wise old man or mentor** (Jung's wise old man; Campbell's mentor or supernatural aid) is the wisdom of tradition: a head start, not a destination. This links to the traditions beat.
- **The villain** is the problem given a face: a large problem, often shaped by and relative to the hero's culture, and genuinely hard.
- **The hero** is whoever can still take that problem on, whatever parochial situation they find themselves in and whatever costume the evil wears.

In the video, the generalities appear briefly in beat 2, as evidence for the theory rather than a tour of the monomyth: departure, ordeal and return; mentor and villain.

The myths are themselves conjectures about what a hero is, and they improve over time: Gilgamesh to K is a storytelling tradition correcting its own picture of heroism.

## Older ideas reaching for the Hero

Jung and Campbell saw something real and got the mechanism wrong.

- **Jung** saw one figure recurring across cultures and explained it as an inherited archetype in the collective unconscious.
- **Campbell** mapped how it recurs in stories. He studied the map.
- **This theory** describes the territory: the Hero is an objective truth about how minds correct errors, and it keeps being rediscovered.

**Convergent evolution.** Eyes evolved separately in octopuses and vertebrates; wings evolved separately in insects, birds and bats. They recur because the problems of seeing and flying are real, and good solutions to real problems look alike. Heroes recur across cultures and universes for the same reason. Jung explained the recurrence by inheritance; this theory explains it by convergence on the truth.

**Why it recurs.** The Hero is the best solution to solving problems reliably, and, in the title of one of Popper's last books, *all life is problem solving*. Candidate line: "The Hero recurs because it is the best solution to the one problem every life faces: how to solve problems."

## What the Hero is: levels

The word works at several levels, as "gene" does in Dawkins: one strand of DNA, or the information spread across every copy.

1. **A copy.** The heroic ideas as instantiated in one mind. Every copy varies.
2. **A hero.** The person who carries a copy and acts on it.
3. **The lineage.** All the copies within one universe: one object made of many instances.
4. **The multiversal object.** The same information lined up across universes. Deutsch argues knowledge is what stays alike across universes while everything else varies at random.
5. **The truth.** The Hero as it really is, which every copy approximates and none fully reaches.

**Realism, not Platonism.** There is a truth of the matter about the knowledge, values and institutions a mind needs to solve problems reliably, resist anti-rational ideas and recover when it falls to them. It holds even if no one has fully grasped or embodied it, much as Popper's World 3 holds that problems and solutions exist before anyone finds them. It is not a finished form: as in physics, every improvement opens new problems, so the approach never ends.

**A direction, unlike genes.** Genes have no target. The Hero's lineage is selected by criticism, so it moves towards the truth.

## The criterion: an uncorrupted core

A hero's reliability is in correcting errors, not avoiding them. Even the greatest hero of an age can fall and make moral mistakes. A person remains a hero through a fall as long as the core ideas that make error-correction reliable are not infected by the rot. The fall becomes real when the rot reaches the core: when an idea starts protecting itself from criticism and the means of noticing the error is lost. This is exactly what the antagonist in the story attacks.

**The tension.** The core cannot be shielded from criticism either, or it becomes dogma. Bartley's answer, which Popper never accepted, is that everything stays open to criticism, including the commitment to criticism itself (F9). The core survives by being the best idea under criticism, not by being untouchable.

**Judging heroism is like judging truth.** Hero status is relative to a truth no one fully knows. We can never certify someone as a hero, but we can often tell when someone has fallen: their ideas have sealed themselves off from criticism.

**A theory, not a definition.** Every concept, like "planet" or "gene", is defined relative to our best theory, and Popper warned against arguing about words. So this is a fallible theory of heroism, and the video can present it openly as a conjecture, which is heroic by its own standard.

## Parochial and universal heroes

Two distinctions make one picture: hero versus non-hero (is the core there) and parochial versus universal (how far it reaches). Hero status is judged domain by domain; universality is how many domains the core reaches. A parochial hero is a hero in some domains and a non-hero in others, like the brilliant scientist who is dogmatic about everything else.

**Why the capital-H Hero must be universal.** Knowledge connects unpredictably, so a domain walled off from criticism does not stay walled off: its errors leak into the rest. The restriction is itself an error waiting to spread. This is the jump to universality applied to character.

**The virtues.** Universal heroes instantiate the values that let them take on new problems: courage to enter the unfamiliar, humility to admit error once there. On this theory the virtues are knowledge too, so the account stays unified.

**Most people are heroes somewhere.** Most people are heroes in at least a narrow domain. The greatest heroes of an age, of myth and of story transform important problems because they face unfamiliar situations and stay heroic through them, growing and learning throughout.

## Evil, scale and recognition

**The tension.** For Popper and Deutsch, problems are things to enjoy and fall in love with. In Campbell's myths, the problem is something evil. The bridge is Deutsch's argument that all evils are due to a lack of knowledge: a villain is a problem experienced as suffering. A plague, a tyrant or death is still a problem, one that hurts while it stays unsolved.

**Two kinds of villain.** One is an evil that causes suffering. The other attacks the means of solving problems at all: a dogma or force that shuts down criticism. The second is the story's antagonist.

**The hero's gift is seeing the dragon as a soluble problem.** The hero creates the knowledge that defeats the dragon, and then it is no longer really a problem. Key line for beat 2.

**Every scale, one core.** The Hero works on every problem, joyful or evil, little or big. What evil changes is the difficulty of the test. A fun puzzle needs curiosity and criticism; an evil, which hurts, frightens and tempts one to give up, also demands hope and courage. Evil is where the whole core is needed at once, which is why stories put a dragon in front of the hero.

**Two senses of hero: objective and recognised.** Objectively, the core is there or not, measured against the truth. In recognition, we call someone a hero when they stand apart from the norm, measured against the crowd: as height is a fact but tallness depends on who stands nearby. Recognition is parochial; the Hero is universal.

**More heroes, less noticed.** If the Hero is objective, a culture that learns these ideas should have more heroes, not fewer, and each will stand out less. A hero is most needed and most noticed where their culture has little of the Hero. In a dogmatic culture, simply asking a question looks heroic. Brecht's *Life of Galileo*: "Unhappy the land that needs heroes" (wording to verify). The aim of a heroic tradition is to make heroism ordinary.

**The frontier always moves.** Even in a more heroic culture, the heroes we notice stand out relative to the problems that culture faces. Problems never run out, and a culture with more knowledge reaches new problems at its frontier. They need not be harder or more evil; they might be, but which they will be cannot be known in advance. So heroism can become ordinary at the base while a future hero still stands out from an already heroic crowd. As in science: every physics student now knows more than Newton did, yet some physicists still stand apart. There is always a beginning of infinity left to stand out in.

## Heroes and heroic acts

Being a hero is the stable instantiation of the heroic ideas; a heroic act is their expression. Because every person is a universal explainer, someone with few heroic ideas can create that knowledge in a moment and act heroically, before their usual ideas override it again: a copy that flickers into existence but does not stick. This is why stories love the unlikely hero.

**The logical form.** Every hero performs heroic acts, but not every heroic act comes from a hero. Being a hero is sufficient for heroic acts but not necessary. Heroes perform them reliably; non-heroes only by chance.

The same applies over a life: a person can become a hero early, fall from the path, or find it late. Guts falls into revenge and turns back; K finds heroism late with no destiny; Gilgamesh corrects his question only at the end.

## Heroes and traditions

Heroes and traditions drive progress together. Individuals belong to traditions and also start new ones, and traditions raise the next heroes. A tradition of criticism may be the Hero living outside any one mind.

The same structure exists at two scales: institutions across individuals (traditions, communities working together) and institutions within one mind (habits, conscience, how it handles its own errors). A mind is a society of ideas.

## Script draft v1

### 1. Hook · The Question

The mythologist Joseph Campbell said the hero has a thousand faces. And decades before him, Carl Jung traced the same recurring figure through the myths of cultures all over the world. They were right: there is a pattern. But the patterns they outlined were far too narrow. They were **parochial**, not universal. The hero's variety is not a thousand. It is **infinite**. Every face is a parochial costume. Beneath them all is the same core: knowledge of how to solve problems, reliably.

### 2. Map and territory · The Map

Stories are maps of heroism. This is about the **territory**. On Campbell's map, the hero leaves home, survives an ordeal and returns changed. Look closer, and each stage is a shadow. Leaving home is facing a problem no one has solved. The ordeal is the **error**. The return is bringing new knowledge back. The figures are shadows too. The wise old mentor is the wisdom of tradition: a head start, not a destination. The villain is the problem, given a face.

*The hero's gift is to see the dragon as a soluble problem.* Then to create the knowledge that defeats it, until it is no longer a problem at all.

### 3. Reaching for the Hero · Convergence

Jung explained the recurrence by inheritance: an archetype buried in all of us. There is a better explanation. Eyes evolved separately in octopuses and in us. Wings evolved separately in insects, birds and bats. They recur because seeing and flying are real problems, and good solutions to real problems look alike. Heroes recur for the same reason. They are **rediscovered**, not inherited.

### 4. The Hero is knowledge · Popper & Deutsch

So what is the Hero? Not a plot. Not an archetype. **Knowledge.** Hope that problems are soluble. The humility to know you are fallible. Curiosity. A critical attitude. The courage to enter the unfamiliar. Running in a mind, that knowledge keeps one cycle turning: a problem, a guess, an error, a correction. Karl Popper said all life is problem solving. David Deutsch calls every person a universal explainer. The Hero is that explainer at its best.

### 5. The levels · Infinite Faces

Think of it the way Richard Dawkins thinks of a gene: not one strand of DNA, but every copy of it in the world. One copy of the Hero lives in one mind. The person who carries it is a hero. All the copies in one world make a **lineage**, each different, each recognisable. And if Deutsch is right that there are many universes, knowledge is what stays the same across them. The worlds scatter. The Hero lines up. Above it all is the truth: the Hero as it really is. Every copy approaches it. None arrives.

*Every answer opens new problems, so the approach never ends.*

### 6. Reliable, not infallible · Guts

Even the greatest hero falls. Guts loses everything and answers with revenge. It is an error, and it nearly consumes him. A hero's reliability is not in avoiding mistakes, moral ones included. It is in **correcting** them. A hero stays a hero through the fall as long as the core survives: the part that can still notice it is wrong. The real fall comes when an idea seals itself off from criticism.

### 7. Open to criticism · No Exceptions

But the core cannot be walled off either. A core that protects itself from criticism is just another **dogma**. So everything stays open to criticism. The hero's methods. The hero's values. Even the nature of the Hero itself.

*This is not a definition. It is a theory, and it could be wrong.*

### 8. Parochial and universal · Nausicaä

Most heroes are parochial. A brilliant scientist can be a hero in the lab and a dogmatist everywhere else. But knowledge connects in ways no one can predict. A domain walled off from criticism does not stay walled off: its errors leak into the rest. So the true Hero is **universal**. Nausicaä does not just defend her valley. She studies the forest everyone else wants to burn, and finds the poison was in the soil.

### 9. Heroes and heroic acts · K

Every hero performs heroic acts. But not every heroic act comes from a hero. K learns he is no one in particular. No prophecy. No destiny. He does the heroic thing **anyway**. A person can find the path late, fall from it, or flicker onto it for one moment before old ideas take over again. That is why stories love the unlikely hero.

### 10. Traditions · Aragorn, Frodo & Sam

No hero corrects **alone**. Aragorn inherits his ancestor's failure, and grows up under the name Estel: hope. Frodo fails at the fire, and the quest survives because of mercy shown long before. Heroes start traditions, and traditions raise the next heroes. A tradition of criticism may be the Hero living outside any one mind. And one mind is a tradition too: a society of ideas.

### 11. Turn to the viewer · You

Most people are heroes somewhere. We notice the ones who stand apart from their culture. In a dogmatic culture, simply asking a question looks heroic. Brecht's Galileo said it best: *unhappy the land that needs heroes.* A culture that learns these ideas has more heroes, and notices each one less. But the **frontier** keeps moving. New problems keep arriving, and no one can know what kind. There is always room to stand out. The greatest heroes transform the problems that matter, in territory no one has mapped.

### 12. Close · Infinite Faces

Campbell saw a thousand faces. Behind them is one Hero, and its faces never run out.

*The Hero recurs because it is the best solution to the one problem every life faces: how to solve problems.*

And one of those faces could be **yours**.

### What the prologue style does

- **Feeling before facts.** It opens on a few short, half-whispered lines of loss.
- **Legend told as history.** Past tense, measured declaratives, an origin told as if it happened.
- **A ceremonial list.** Peoples or gifts named in turn, building scale and fate.
- **The turn.** One short sentence overturns what came before.
- **The fade.** What mattered slips from history into legend and is nearly lost.
- **It ends small.** The great thing waits for the unlikeliest bearer, and the last line points at the present.

The video takes the devices, not the words: no line of the film is quoted or echoed.

### Shared rules

- **Earn every lift.** Claim, then evidence, then the grand line; never the grand line instead of the argument.
- **Rhythm.** Two longer sentences, then a short one. End each beat on a short one.
- **Concrete nouns over big adjectives.** Fever, flood, firelight; not "immense" or "profound".
- **Repetition only to build.** Never as padding.
- **Grandeur from scale.** Ages, worlds and libraries carry the epic feel without help.
- **Keep the fact-check hedges.** "Deutsch argues", "if Deutsch is right".

### Delivery

The prologue lines need a slower read (Kokoro speed about 0.85 against 0.93 for the rest), longer pauses and the music rising under them. A low, calm voice suits the whole piece; one of Kokoro's British female voices is the nearest placeholder, and a real recording will do it far better.

## Script v2

The narration to record: 918 words in 13 beats, about 6 minutes 10 seconds at draft 2's measured pace (1,150 words ran 7:30), with the slower prologue included. Each sentence is one narration line. **Bold** is the on-screen bold word, *italic* lines render in italics, and the register is named under each heading. It replaces script draft v1, which stays above for comparison.

### 0. Cold open · Prologue

*Register: elegiac prologue. Read slowly, with long pauses.*

Before the first story was written, there was a fire. Not the fire of the forge or the hearth, but a smaller one, kindled in a single mind: the thought that the darkness could be understood.

It passed from hand to hand. To the hunter who learned the herd. To the healer who learned the fever. To the child who asked *why*, and would not be silenced.

Kingdoms forgot it. Empires burned the books that held it. And always, somewhere, someone found it again.

They called the ones who carried it heroes. They gave them a thousand faces.

*They were wrong about the number.*

### 1. Hook · The Question

*Register: humanist scripture.*

The mythologist Joseph Campbell said the hero has a thousand faces. Decades before him, Carl Jung traced the same figure through the myths of the world. And they were right: there is a pattern. But the patterns they drew were **parochial**, not universal. The hero's variety is not a thousand. It is **infinite**. Every face is a costume, made for one place and one time. Beneath them all is one core: the knowledge of how to solve problems, reliably.

### 2. Map and territory · The Map

*Register: mythmaker's clarity.*

Stories are maps of heroism. This is about the **territory**. On Campbell's map, the hero leaves home, survives the ordeal and returns changed. Each stage is a shadow. Leaving home is meeting a problem no one has solved. The ordeal is the **error**. The return is new knowledge, carried back. The mentor is the wisdom of tradition. The villain is the problem, given a face.

*The hero's gift is to see the dragon as a soluble problem.* To learn where it sleeps, why it burns, and make the knowledge that defeats it, until it is no problem at all.

### 3. Reaching for the Hero · Convergence

*Register: cosmic rationalist.*

Jung blamed inheritance: an archetype buried in us all. There is a better explanation. Eyes evolved separately in octopuses and in us. Wings evolved separately in insects, birds and bats. Seeing and flying are real problems, and good solutions to real problems look alike. The hero recurs for the same reason. Not inherited. **Rediscovered.**

### 4. The Hero is knowledge · Popper & Deutsch

*Register: cosmic rationalist.*

So what is the Hero? Not a plot. Not an archetype. **Knowledge.** Hope that problems are soluble. Humility about your own errors. Curiosity. Criticism. The courage to walk into the unknown. Running in a mind, it turns one cycle: a problem, a guess, an error, a correction. Karl Popper said all life is problem solving. David Deutsch calls every person a universal explainer. The Hero is that explainer at its best.

### 5. The levels · Infinite Faces

*Register: labyrinth essay. Plays over the stack sequence.*

Think of it as Richard Dawkins thinks of a gene: not one strand, but every copy, everywhere. One copy of the Hero lives in one mind. All the copies in one world form a **lineage**, each different, each recognisable. And if Deutsch is right that there are many worlds, knowledge is what stays the same across them. The worlds scatter. The Hero lines up. Above it all is the truth: the Hero as it really is. Every copy reaches for it. None arrives.

*Every answer opens new questions. The climb has no top.*

### 6. The fall · Guts

*Register: stark defiance.*

Even the greatest hero falls. Guts loses everything, and answers with revenge. It is an error, and it nearly eats him alive. A hero's strength is not in never falling. It is in **correcting**, moral errors too. A hero stays a hero while the core survives: the part that can still say, *I was wrong*. The real fall comes when an idea seals itself off from criticism.

### 7. Open to criticism · No Exceptions

*Register: stark defiance.*

So the core cannot be sealed either. A core beyond question is just another **dogma**. Everything stays open. The hero's methods. The hero's values. Even the nature of the Hero.

*This is not a definition. It is a theory. It could be wrong.*

### 8. Parochial and universal · Nausicaä

*Register: cosmic rationalist.*

Most heroes are parochial. A scientist can be a hero in the lab and a dogmatist everywhere else. But knowledge connects in ways no one can predict. No wall against criticism holds: the errors leak through. So the true Hero is **universal**. Nausicaä does not just defend her valley. She studies the forest everyone else wants to burn, and finds the poison was in the soil.

### 9. Heroes and heroic acts · K

*Register: mythmaker's clarity.*

Every hero performs heroic acts. Not every heroic act comes from a hero. K learns he is no one in particular. No prophecy. No destiny. He does the heroic thing **anyway**. A person can find the path late, lose it, or stand on it for one moment before the old ideas return. That is why stories love the unlikely hero.

### 10. Traditions · Aragorn, Frodo & Sam

*Register: mythmaker's clarity.*

And no hero corrects **alone**. Aragorn inherits his ancestor's failure, and is raised under another name: Estel. Hope. Frodo fails at the fire, and the quest is saved by a mercy shown long before. Heroes begin traditions. Traditions raise heroes. A tradition of criticism may be the Hero living outside any one mind.

### 11. Turn to the viewer · The Frontier

*Register: cosmic rationalist.*

Most people are heroes somewhere. We only notice the ones who stand apart. In a dogmatic land, asking a question is heroic. Brecht's Galileo put it plainly: *unhappy the land that needs heroes.* A culture that learns these ideas has more heroes, and notices each one less. But the **frontier** moves. New problems arrive, and no one can know their kind. There is always an edge of the map. The greatest heroes cross it.

### 12. Close · Infinite Faces

*Register: elegiac prologue into humanist scripture.*

Campbell saw a thousand faces. Behind them is one Hero, and its faces never run out.

*The Hero recurs because it is the best solution to the one problem every life faces: how to solve problems.*

The fire is still passing from hand to hand. Somewhere, someone is finding it again.

It could be **you**.

## Visual direction

Draft 3 keeps draft 2's dark archival collage and scrolling serif column, and adds one through-line: a single ember of light that is passed from hand to hand in the cold open, becomes the Hero's mark, forms the column in the stack, and ends in Doré's light. The video moves from near-black to light over its 6½ minutes, and each writing register gets its own visual mode.

### Principles

1. **One continuous collage.** As in the reference shorts, the frame evolves rather than cuts. Only four hard cuts: into the title (end of beat 0), into the stack (beat 5), to black (end of beat 7), and to the end card.
2. **The ember is the Hero.** A small warm point of light (ember gold) appears in every beat, usually on or near the hero image. It is the visual form of the knowledge, so it never belongs to one character.
3. **Darkness to light.** The grade starts near-black and warms beat by beat; beats 6–7 dip back into darkness; beat 12 is the brightest frame in the film.
4. **The mark is an open ring with an arrowhead.** The cycle drawn as one stroke, never closed. It first appears as Campbell's circle being opened (beat 2) and becomes the cycle diagram (beat 4) and the column (beat 5).
5. **Archive first.** Public-domain engravings carry every idea; copyrighted heroes appear only while the narration discusses them, mid-size, cut into collage pieces.

### Type

- **Body lines:** EB Garamond on warm white strips, one bold word per sentence, as in draft 2.
- **Italic lines become inscriptions:** a black strip with warm white italic text, held 1.2 s longer, so the epigrams read as carved lines apart from the argument.
- **Title and end card:** condensed serif, last word in italics ("The Hero With Infinite *Faces*").
- **Prologue:** centred italic, no strips, fading in line by line.

### Sound

The music follows the light: sparse in the prologue, building through the stack in beat 5, falling almost to nothing in beats 6–7, rebuilding through beats 8–11, and swelling at the close. Two silences carry weight: 1.5 s of near-silence before "This is not a definition" and 1 s before "It could be you".
